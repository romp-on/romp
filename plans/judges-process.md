# Stage three of the process split: the judges in their own process

**Status:** MEASURED AND CLOSED 2026-09-18 (the read is below): the judges stay in the kernel, the default as it stands; the child (`romp-judge --serve`, PRs 1745, 1758 and 1850, kernel switch `STATE/judges-process`, clock file `STATE/judges-process-clock`, PR 1854) stays as an opt-in diagnostic, with no further build on this road. Written 2026-09-15 by romp_perf as a design line for a read before code; the judges' side romp_metrics's with romp_perf; the read romp_manager's. Stage three of plans/process-split.md.

## Why now

The kernel is one interpreter on effectively one core, and the judges share it. The four-reviewer read of 2026-09-14 measured, over a 617 second window at a boot with a browser attached, 422 seconds of judge tier wall, 116 seconds of tier CPU and 37 seconds of worker CPU, beside the refresh thread's 526 seconds and the housekeeping's 394; the rest of the judges' wall is time in model subprocesses, which costs the interpreter nothing. Tonight's idle boot (2026-09-15, 367 seconds up, no client connected) read the process at 104.7 CPU seconds, of which the judges' 85 passes took 35.9: a third of an idle kernel's CPU. Stage one and one b have landed (a saved document for every session with two settled turns), so the judges' parses are restores and folds, not cold decodes (at a fresh boot: restore 27, fold 11, full 0); what they still pay on the shared interpreter is the fold work and the tree walks after, and every second of it is a second the refresh thread waits when a browser is redialing. The cold redial of the largest tab took 45 to 68 seconds on tonight's boots under that contention.

## The premise, checked in code

- **The judges are a loop in the kernel.** `_producer` (kernel.py) waits up to `BOOT_JUDGE_HOLD_S` (8 s) for the boot's attaches, then loops on `_producer_wake` (set by `POST /tick`, by a feed connect and by the postal bus; a 3 s backstop). Each pass: `_episode_boundary_tick`, `_begin_goals_pass` (the pre-pass goal stores snapshotted so `build_feed` serves a pass-consistent view), `jd.begin_pass_frame` (one evidence frame for both tiers and their pools), two tier threads (`jd.run_index`: captioner and archiver; `jd.run_triage`: planner, closer, unblocker, courier, propagate, grouper, consolidator, distiller), a barrier, `jd.end_pass_frame`, `_compact_goal_stores` (the single writer archiving cleared tops after the join), `jd.consume_judge_recovery` and `jd.rearm_failed_summaries`, `_end_goals_pass`, `_bump_judge_gen_if_changed` (the view signatures refresh only when a judge-written store moved), the scheduled sweep of the SDK backend, and `_PerfStats.judge_pass`. The master switch `_task_tracking_on()` starts no tier when off.
- **The judges read files and write files.** `jd.discover` walks `names/` behind a directory-mtime fingerprint; the tiers read transcripts, the states logs and the postal log through `em.parse_session`, the goal stores through `jd.load_goals`, and write captions (`CAPDIR`), goal trees (`GOALDIR`), episodes (`EPIDIR`), archives and the cleared archive: every input and output is a file under the state directory. The kernel's feed reads the goal stores from their files (`load_goals_shared_or_fault` reads a store file when one exists). Nothing a judge decides reaches the kernel through memory except the pass frame and the perf counters.
- **Each judge is already a subprocess.** A judge call is `claude -p` under a `perl alarm` (`_judge_claude_bin`, `CALL_ALARM_S`), fanned through `_TimedPool` executors of `_judge_concurrency()` workers (the gear's setting, 1 to 16, read from the state directory each pass), and the module already runs standalone (`romp-judge --once`, `--plan`, `--close`, and the tests' fake `claude` through `ROMP_CLAUDE_BIN`).
- **What the kernel keeps for itself.** Four housekeeping jobs on the jobs thread read `jd.parsed_session` directly for their turns: the interrupt block, the closer's pending check, the awaiting wake outcomes and the deferral sweep. They need the answer in the same call; they stay in the kernel with the module they already load, and with documents each costs a restore (0.2 s for the largest transcript) on a miss, not a cold decode.
- **Memory is one pool.** The kernel's record cache budget is a fraction of MemTotal (`recordCache.budgetBytes`); a second process holding parsed trees takes its share from the same pool.
- **The free-threaded interpreter is closed.** Measured in plans/process-split.md: slower on one thread and no better on six.

## The rule

1. **One long-lived child, one pass per request.** The kernel starts `romp-judge --serve` as a plain child of its own, lazily at the first pass the switch sends to it (not at boot, and not in a transient scope: the child must die with the kernel, which a scope would outlive, and on Linux it also asks for the parent-death signal; the kernel's environment, state directory and `ROMP_CLAUDE_BIN` inherited), and speaks a line protocol over the child's stdin and stdout: one `pass` line per producer wake carrying `now` and `mayStart`, the in-process gate's composite (`_tiers_may_start`: the tracking switch, a live session, retries not paused) evaluated by the kernel, an absent field false; the child runs both tiers under its own pass frame and its own parse store, writes the stores as today, and answers one `done` line carrying the counters the kernel's `/perf` judge block reads today (pass wall, tier starts, worker CPU, tier CPU, failures) plus the child's own record cache figures. A per-pass subprocess is not the shape: it would rebuild the parse store from documents every pass, tens of restores a pass.
2. **The kernel keeps its bookkeeping around the request.** `_episode_boundary_tick` before; `_begin_goals_pass` before and `_end_goals_pass` after (they gate the kernel's own feed reads); `_compact_goal_stores`, the recovery re-arm and `_bump_judge_gen_if_changed` after the child's `done`; the SDK scheduled sweep as today. The order the loop has now is the order around the request; the pass frame moves into the child whole.
3. **The child dies loud and comes back.** A pass has a bound (the child's own per-pass budget today, plus a hard bound in the kernel of a small multiple of it); a child that answers nothing by the hard bound is killed and restarted, the pass counted `judge.passesLost` and a line written; a child that exits is restarted on the next wake, counted `judge.childRestarts` on `/perf` and on the boot row. The kernel's restart restarts the child (a deploy already restarts everything). A malformed `done` line is a lost pass, never a silent accept.
4. **The switch, the knobs and the failure scan stay where they are.** `_task_tracking_on()` off, no live session or retries paused: the kernel sends `mayStart` false (or no `pass` at all), the child starts no tier, no kernel-initiated model call, as today. The pass body between the frame's open and end is one function in kernel/judge.py (`run_pass`) that the in-process producer and the child both call, so neither can drift from the other during the rollout. The concurrency setting is read by the child from the same state file each pass. `jd.judge_failure_scan` and the recovery edge read files and stay in the kernel. The recovery edge is the child's own: `consume_judge_recovery` reads the in-process call-health flag, which the child's calls set in the child, so the child consumes it per pass and the `done` line carries `recovered`, on which the kernel re-arms the given-up cards (the kernel head's round two).
5. **Rollout in two steps.** The first cut lands the child with a kernel switch defaulting to the in-process loop, so the two can be measured on the same box across boots; the second flips the default once the boot measurement below holds and removes the in-process loop.

## The read (2026-09-18)

Five windows on the devbox with the switch written at each boundary and no kernel restart of the measurement's own; the switch is read at every pass. Two windows ran with the team's suites on the box and are confounded (read on counts only); three ran in a quiet window the manager held (7:00 to 9:00 PM Pacific, no suites, labs or builds), forty minutes each on one boot: the judges in the kernel, the child with the wake's time in each request, the child with a null clock (a deploy fell inside the third, which is read from that boot, 24 minutes). The quiet figures, in that order: passes per hour 1000, 1012 and 1002 (the cadence is the producer's wake, not the mode); wall per pass 659, 635 and 663 ms; kernel judge CPU per pass 234, 257 and 260 ms, with 51 to 56 ms of child worker CPU per pass on top of the child's; model calls per pass 0.04 in all three; tier starts per pass 2.0 in all three; passes lost 0; one child restart in the request window; kernel RSS 2394 MB at the end of the in-process window and 2601 MB at the end of the child window forty minutes later on the same boot, with the child at 1442 MB beside it. The `tierGate` block's `due_clock` read 0 in every child pass under both clocks. The decision, the user's (2026-09-18, who wanted the empirical best): the child bought nothing measurable (the pass rate, the wall, the calls and the outcomes are the in-process figures) and spent 1.3 to 1.4 GB of a shared pool, so the judges stay in the kernel; the clock variant is moot, so the null default stands and the request file stays a diagnostic. The premise above stands as written (the judges' interpreter share is real); what the measurement says is that moving the passes out did not shorten them or lighten the kernel, because the pass is bounded by its wake and its model calls, not by the interpreter, and the child's parse store duplicates the kernel's rather than replacing it. An earlier reading of two boots that found the child slower (0.62 model calls per pass against 0.25) was the box's load, not the mode: under the quiet window the calls per pass are equal. The rule's step five (the flip of the default and the removal of the in-process loop) does not happen.

## What the user sees after this

A browser redialing during a boot no longer waits for the judges' folds and tree walks on the same interpreter: the first refresh and the connect pushes are bounded by the kernel's own work. The judges keep their cadence, their stores and their gear settings; a judge failure surfaces the same way, and a child that dies says so on the boot row.

## The measurement

On the devbox, with the whole-chat-frames switch back off and a browser attached at the boot, read from `/perf` and the boot row across the next boots with the switch on and off: the first refresh's `push.chat` and `pusher.connectPush.byApp` (chat, feed, timeline) against tonight's 45 to 68 second cold redials; `pusher.cycle_ms_p50` and `cycle_ms_p90` during judge passes; `process.cpu_s` split into the kernel's and the child's; `judge.ms_sum` and `judge.cpu_ms_workers` reported by the child against the in-process figures. The claim to hold: the connect pushes fall by the judges' interpreter share during the first minute, and the judges' pass cadence and outcomes are unchanged.

## Tests (red first at main)

- A hermetic kernel over the tests' fake `claude` runs one pass through the child: the stores the pass writes are the in-process pass's (the same fixture, the same files), the `done` line's counters land on `/perf`'s judge block, and the kernel's bookkeeping runs in its order around the request (the goals snapshot open across the pass and closed after, the generation bumped only when a store moved).
- The failure roads: a child that exits mid-pass (the pass counted lost, a restart on the next wake, the row's count); a child that hangs past the hard bound (killed, restarted, counted); a malformed `done` line (lost, never accepted); tracking off sends no request.
- The switch: with the default off, the in-process loop runs as today and no child starts; on, the child runs and the loop does not.

## Roads not taken

- **Threads.** The tiers already run on threads; the interpreter is the bottleneck, and the free-threaded build measured slower.
- **A subprocess per pass.** Simple, but every pass rebuilds the parse store: at a fresh boot tonight the judges took 27 restores and 11 folds in one pass, and a restore of the largest transcript is 0.2 s; a long-lived child keeps its store warm across passes.
- **Moving the kernel's own parse readers into the child.** The four jobs need their answer in the same call on the jobs thread; a round trip per call would add latency to housekeeping for a saving the documents already made small.
- **A socket instead of a pipe.** No second machine needs to reach the judges; the pipe is the kernel's own child, dies with it, and needs no token.
- **The judges' process first without the refold cut.** The rewound-uuid refold (a 282 MB whole read once per boot, pinned by romp_metrics to a redundant one-file guard in `_per_file_rewound`) is a cheaper saving on the same first pass and lands on its own, before or beside this line.
